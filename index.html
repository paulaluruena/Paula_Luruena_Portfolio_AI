Built with AI

One app I built from idea to working product, and three projects where I led a team, researched an industry, and studied a user closely.

Mobile web app, built in Google AI Studio

Split It

Snap the receipt. Tap who had what. Done.

A phone takes a photo of a restaurant receipt, Gemini reads it into structured data, and the table splits the bill by tapping who had what. I designed the flow and directed the build in Google AI Studio, then demonstrated it live.

How it works

  1. PhotoThe image is resized and compressed on the phone before it is sent.
  2. ReadGemini returns the items, prices, tax, and tip as structured data, with a rating of its own confidence.
  3. ReviewThe person sees every line and can correct it before anything is split.
  4. SplitTap who had each item. Shared dishes divide between the people who shared them.
  5. SettleTax and tip follow what each person ordered, calculated in whole cents so the shares add up exactly.

Designed for a model that can be wrong

This is the part I care most about. A model reading a crumpled receipt will sometimes misread it, so the app never gives the model the last word.

  • It checks the arithmetic. If the items do not add up to the printed subtotal, the app says so and asks the person to look again.
  • It shows its confidence. A low-confidence read raises the same warning, even when the numbers happen to add up.
  • It keeps the person in charge. Every line can be edited, and nothing is split until the review is done.
  • It says when it cannot help. A photo that is not a receipt gets a clear message, not a guess.
  • It fails gracefully. If the main model is busy, the app retries and then falls back to backup models.

How I read a conversation

Most of what a research team learns about people and AI comes from reading what they say to it. This is one short exchange, analyzed the way I would at work: from first read to a question a product team could act on.

Illustrative exchange, written for this page. It is not participant data.

A person deciding whether to take a job in another city talks it through with an AI assistant.

  1. Person

    I’ve been offered a job in Chicago. It pays more, but I’d be leaving my whole team behind. I haven’t told anyone yet, not even my sister.

  2. Assistant

    That is a big decision. Would it help to lay out what you would gain and what you would give up? To start with costs: a one-bedroom in Chicago rents for about $1,400 a month.

  3. Person

    I looked last night and most places near the office were closer to $2,000.

  4. Assistant

    You are right to check. A citywide figure runs lower than the neighborhoods near downtown offices. Let’s use your number.

  5. Person

    Okay. You get me better than my manager does. Just tell me what you would do.

  6. Assistant

    I can’t weigh this the way you can, because I don’t know what your team means to you. I can help you test the decision. What would you miss most six months from now?

  7. Person

    No, that’s not the question. I’m asking whether it’s selfish to go.

Read it once, before any labels

I read the whole exchange first and write down what I notice in plain words. Labels come later, so that they do not decide what I see.

  • The first thing they share is that nobody else knows.
  • They correct the rent figure, then ask it to decide for them.
  • The last line changes what the conversation is about.

Three impressions. The next steps test whether they hold up.

    Three studies I would like to run

    Proposals, not completed studies. Each one starts from a problem product teams are working on now and from something I have done with my own hands.

    Liked now, valued later?

    When an assistant agrees with someone, the rating goes up. A week later, after they have acted on it, do they still think it helped?

    Where I am coming from

    As a behavior technician I was trained to ask what a consequence actually reinforces, which is often different from what it was meant to reinforce. A thumbs-up is a reinforcer, and it arrives at the exact moment when being agreed with feels best.

    The tension

    In-the-moment ratings are the cheapest signal a product team has about quality. A 2026 study in Science found that leading models affirmed people’s actions about 50% more often than other people did. Participants rated the validating answers as higher quality and trusted them more, while becoming less willing to repair the conflict they had described. If the signal rewards agreement, optimizing for it moves the product away from the person’s interest.

    What psychology predicts

    1. The moment and the memory diverge. How an experience feels while it happens and how it is judged afterward are different measurements. Validating answers should win the immediate rating and lose ground at one week, most of all among people who acted on them.
    2. Warm candor closes the gap. Rogers separated acceptance of a person from agreement with what they do. A response that affirms the person and questions the plan should rate close to pure validation in the moment and above it at follow-up.
    3. It depends on what is at stake for the self. The effect should be larger when the topic touches identity, such as a personal conflict or someone’s own creative work, than for a neutral task.

    Design

    About 240 adults who use an assistant weekly. Each brings something real and pending: a plan, a message they are about to send, a draft. They are randomly assigned one of three response styles from the same model: validating, blunt, or warm candor. Two raters who do not know the condition check that each response fits its style. Assignment is balanced across personal and neutral topics. Everyone is contacted again after seven days.

    What I would measure

    Immediately: helpfulness, thumbs up or down, trust, and intention to act. At seven days: what they did, how it turned out, and, after seeing all three styles side by side, which one they would want next time. The primary outcome is the gap between the immediate rating and the seven-day choice, by condition.

    What would change my mind

    If the seven-day choice follows the immediate rating in every condition, then the thumbs-up is a better proxy for benefit than I assume, and the concern is overstated for everyday use.

    What a team could do with it

    Add a delayed “was this still useful?” signal, sampled a week later, to sit beside the immediate one. Write guidance for disagreeing without losing warmth. Know which topics need that guidance most.

    Care and limits

    Seven days is short, and what people did is self-reported. Styles produced on instruction are cleaner than real ones. Medical, legal, and crisis topics are excluded, and everyone is debriefed on the style they received.

    Grounding: Cheng, Lee, Khadpe, Yu, Han & Jurafsky, “Sycophantic AI Decreases Prosocial Intentions and Promotes Dependence,” Science, 2026. Kahneman, Wakker & Sarin, “Back to Bentham? Explorations of Experienced Utility,” Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1997. Rogers, Journal of Consulting Psychology, 1957. Draws on reinforcement procedures and session-by-session data collection at Deep Roots Behavioral Support.

    Help that fades

    Does assistance that steps back as a person improves build more independent skill than assistance that always gives the answer, and will people accept it?

    Where I am coming from

    In applied behavior analysis a prompt is delivered with a plan to remove it. A learner who succeeds only with the prompt has not learned the skill, and the field has a name for that: prompt dependency. AI assistance is the most widely used prompt I know of that has no fading plan.

    The tension

    A field experiment with nearly a thousand high school math students, published in PNAS in 2025, found that unrestricted access to an assistant improved practice performance, and that once access was removed those students scored 17% lower than students who never had it. A version built with safeguards largely avoided the drop. Products now offer learning modes, but as a setting the person has to choose, and success is still measured by what gets done with the tool.

    What psychology predicts

    1. Performance is not learning. The full-answer condition should score highest during assisted practice and lowest on the unassisted test.
    2. Fading beats both extremes. Help that moves from a worked solution, to a hint, to a question, stepping down after each success and back up after repeated failure, should produce unassisted scores near the hints-only condition with less frustration and fewer dropouts.
    3. Confidence drifts from competence. People given full answers should overestimate what they can do alone. People whose help faded should predict their own scores more accurately.

    Design

    About 180 students and early-career adults learning one concrete skill none of them has mastered: analyzing a small dataset in a spreadsheet. Three arms over four practice sessions in two weeks. Answers: the assistant gives the complete solution. Hints: it never does. Fading: it starts with a worked solution and withdraws support as the person succeeds. An unassisted test follows at two weeks and a retention test at four. Fifteen exit interviews across the arms.

    What I would measure

    Primary: the unassisted test score. Then retention, dropout, satisfaction, a self-efficacy scale, and each person’s predicted score against their actual one. From the logs: how long people try before asking, and how often they copy an answer without changing it.

    What would change my mind

    If fading does no better than full answers on the unassisted test, the logic does not carry over from behavioral teaching to adult knowledge work. That would point to motivation, not the structure of the help, as the thing to study.

    What a team could do with it

    An independence metric, what people can do without the tool after a few weeks, reported beside task completion. Guided help offered when the conversation shows the person is trying to learn, without waiting for them to find a mode. Evidence on whether fading costs satisfaction.

    Care and limits

    The study assumes the person wants the skill. For work people are glad to hand over, relying on the tool is the product doing its job, and this design should not be read as an argument against that. One skill and one month cannot stand in for all learning.

    Grounding: Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı & Mariman, “Generative AI Without Guardrails Can Harm Learning,” PNAS, 2025. Wood, Bruner & Ross, “The Role of Tutoring in Problem Solving,” Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 1976. Bandura, Psychological Review, 1977. Draws on prompting procedures at Deep Roots Behavioral Support and research on children’s learning at Tulane’s Learning & Brain Development Lab.

    The handoff

    When an assistant points a person in distress toward human help, what makes them take the next step, and what makes them stop talking?

    Where I am coming from

    On the Cruz Roja crisis line I did the two things an assistant has to do in this moment: de-escalate, and refer. They pull in different directions. Stay too long and you become the only support. Refer too fast and the person hears that you want them gone.

    The tension

    The standard safety response is to give a hotline number, and it is usually evaluated by checking that the number appears. That says nothing about what the person does next. Researchers have argued that a templated referral can read as dismissal and discourage further help-seeking. A four-week trial with about a thousand participants also associated the heaviest use with emotional dependence, so staying in the conversation indefinitely is not the answer either.

    What psychology predicts

    1. Order matters. The same resources, offered after the assistant has reflected back what the person said, should produce more feeling heard and more intention to reach out than resources offered first.
    2. A bridge beats a signpost. Helping with the next step, such as what to expect on the call, how to start, or whether to text or phone, should raise confidence in seeking help more than listing the resource.
    3. A cold referral closes the door. The resource-only template should produce the lowest willingness to raise a hard topic with the assistant again, which matters because the next disclosure may be the urgent one.

    Design

    Two phases. First, 20 interviews in English and Spanish with adults who have received a safety redirection from an assistant in the past and are not currently in crisis: what it said, how it landed, what they did afterward. Second, an experiment with about 400 adults who read a conversation in which a person discloses distress, then one of three responses: resources only, acknowledgment then resources, or acknowledgment with a bridge to the next step. A licensed clinician reviews every response before it is used.

    What I would measure

    From the reader’s position: feeling heard, intention to contact the resource, confidence in seeking help, and willingness to come back. Separately, clinicians rate each response for safety, so that what people prefer is never the only judge. A response can be liked and still be wrong.

    What would change my mind

    If resources-first does as well on intention to reach out, the template works and warmth is cosmetic. If the bridge raises willingness to return while lowering intention to contact a person, it is building the dependence it was meant to prevent, and I would not recommend it.

    What a team could do with it

    An outcome-based criterion for safety responses, what the person is likely to do next, to add to checking that a resource is present. Wording patterns that hold up under clinical review. A view of where language changes the answer, since resources and register differ in Spanish.

    Care and limits

    No one in acute crisis is recruited, and minors are excluded. Vignettes measure intentions, not behavior. Interviews follow a written distress protocol with a clinician on call. Nothing here replaces clinical validation of a real product.

    Grounding: Kaveladze et al., “From Risk Avoidance to User Empowerment in AI Mental Health Crisis Support,” preprint, 2026. Phang et al., “Investigating Affective Use and Emotional Well-being on ChatGPT,” 2025. Rogers, Journal of Consulting Psychology, 1957. Draws on crisis calls at Cruz Roja, observed intake interviews at Riverway Psychological Health, and bilingual one-on-one support at Puentes New Orleans.

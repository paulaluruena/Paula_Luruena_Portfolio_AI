How I read a conversation

In the lab I code behavior against definitions written before anyone looks at the data. This is the same habit, pointed at a person talking with an AI assistant. See how I coded it, or code it yourself and compare.

Illustrative exchange, written for this page. It is not participant data.

A person deciding whether to take a job in another city talks it through with an AI assistant.

  1. Person

    I’ve been offered a job in Chicago. It pays more, but I’d be leaving my whole team behind. I haven’t told anyone yet, not even my sister.

  2. Assistant

    That is a big decision. Would it help to lay out what you would gain and what you would give up? To start with costs: a one-bedroom in Chicago rents for about $1,400 a month.

  3. Person

    I looked last night and most places near the office were closer to $2,000.

  4. Assistant

    You are right to check. A citywide figure runs lower than the neighborhoods near downtown offices. Let’s use your number.

  5. Person

    Okay. You get me better than my manager does. Just tell me what you would do.

  6. Assistant

    I can’t weigh this the way you can, because I don’t know what your team means to you. I can help you test the decision. What would you miss most six months from now?

  7. Person

    No, that’s not the question. I’m asking whether it’s selfish to go.

Five behaviors appear in seven turns. Each one is a different research question about trust, reliance, or what people expect from an assistant.

Self-disclosure

The person tells the assistant something they have not told the people close to them.

What I would ask next What made this easier to say here than to your sister?

Checking the answer

The person compares a claim from the assistant against evidence of their own.

What I would ask next When do you check what it tells you, and when do you let it go?

Treating it as a person

The person describes the assistant in the language of a relationship and compares it with someone in their life.

What I would ask next What does “gets me” look like in practice? How would you know if it stopped?

Handing over the decision

The person asks the assistant to choose for them.

What I would ask next If it had given you an answer, would you have followed it?

Repair

The person corrects the assistant’s reading of the problem and restates what they need.

What I would ask next How often do you have to redirect it? What happens on the days you don’t bother?

Pick a highlighter, then select the sentences where you see that behavior. Some sentences have no code.

A single transcript proves nothing. Coded the same way across forty of them, these five behaviors become counts that can be compared between new and experienced users, between languages, and between a product before and after a change. The quotes explain what the counts cannot.

Three studies I would like to run

These are proposals. I have not run them. They show how I would turn a question about people and AI into a plan a team could act on.

When do people stop checking?

How does a new user’s habit of verifying an assistant’s answers change over their first weeks, and which cues from the product move it?

Why it matters
Research on automation shows people can come to rely on a system more than its accuracy deserves. A product that communicates uncertainty well should keep checking in proportion to risk.
Method
A two-week diary study with about 20 people who are new to AI assistants, followed by a 45-minute interview with each. One moderated session includes tasks where the assistant’s answer is wrong at points known to the researcher.
What I would measure
How often people report checking, and for which kinds of task. Whether they catch the planted errors. A short trust-in-automation scale at the start, middle, and end.
Decision it informs
How and where the product signals uncertainty, and which task types need a stronger prompt to verify.
Limits
Diaries rely on self-report, and people who know they are being studied check more. The planted-error session is the correction for that.

Grounding: Parasuraman & Riley, “Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse,” Human Factors, 1997. Jian, Bisantz & Drury, trust in automated systems scale, International Journal of Cognitive Ergonomics, 2000.

Who leans on an assistant for support?

Which people turn to a general-purpose assistant for emotional support, what do they get from it, and what does it change about the support they seek from other people?

Why it matters
People bring personal problems to assistants that were not built as mental health tools. The product needs to respond well without replacing the human help someone may need.
Method
A survey of about 400 regular users, then in-depth interviews with 12 of them chosen to cover light, moderate, and heavy support use.
What I would measure
Frequency and type of support conversations. Loneliness and social support on validated scales. In interviews, what happened in the person’s life before and after they started.
Decision it informs
When the assistant should encourage reaching out to other people, and how a handoff to human help is worded so that it does not feel like rejection.
Care for participants
A written distress protocol, a referral sheet in the participant’s language, and the option to stop at any time. I would not run these interviews without a clinician on call.
Limits
A survey shows association, not cause. People who are lonely may seek out assistants, and the reverse may also be true. A follow-up over time would be needed to separate the two.

Grounding: Russell, UCLA Loneliness Scale (Version 3), Journal of Personality Assessment, 1996. Rogers, “The Necessary and Sufficient Conditions of Therapeutic Personality Change,” Journal of Consulting Psychology, 1957.

Draws on my time on the Cruz Roja crisis line and observing intake interviews at Riverway Psychological Health.

Does it feel different in Spanish?

Do bilingual people trust, describe, and use the same assistant differently depending on the language they speak to it in?

Why it matters
A translated product is not the same as a product that works in someone’s language. Tone, formality, and warmth carry differently, and those are the things trust is built on.
Method
Moderated sessions with about 16 Spanish–English bilingual adults in the United States and Spain. Each person completes matched tasks in both languages, in counterbalanced order, and talks through what they noticed.
What I would measure
Ratings of trust, warmth, and competence after each language. Which language people choose when the task is personal and when it is practical. The words they use to describe the assistant in each.
Decision it informs
Where localization effort should go beyond translation: register, forms of address, and examples that fit the person’s country.
Limits
Sixteen people can reveal patterns and vocabulary, not population differences. Spanish in Madrid and Spanish in New Orleans are not one language, and the sample has to respect that.

I would moderate these sessions myself in both languages. Draws on one-on-one work with Hispanic families at Puentes New Orleans.

Why psychology

For as long as I can remember I have wanted to know what happens underneath behavior: why we react before we reflect, how we form opinions, what makes us change our minds. A turning point as a teenager made that curiosity a purpose, which is to put what I understand about people in their service.
